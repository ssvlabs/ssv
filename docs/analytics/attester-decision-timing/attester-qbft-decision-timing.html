Attester QBFT decides late — and it barely matters

SSV runs one QBFT per committee‑slot for attestations. Its round‑1 timer runs to 6s (a 4s head‑start), so committee decisions land late by design — 30% after 3s. Unlike a block proposal, that lateness costs almost nothing: a decided attestation still lands on‑chain essentially always. The only casualty is head‑vote quality, and only right at the 4s deadline.

Phase 1 · when the committee decides

Late by design — the committee timer allows 6s

Committee decision time (Exporter's aggregated‑decided message, into slot) peaks at ~2.5s and spreads to 4–6s, because the committee round‑1 timer is slot‑anchored at 6s (a slotDuration/3 head‑start), vs the proposer's 2s. So a third of committee duties decide after 3s and 8% after the 4s attestation deadline — timings that would be catastrophic for a proposal.

Phase 1 · does it hurt?

A decided attestation always lands; only head‑vote quality dips, right at 4s

Every one of 1.84M decided committee‑attestations was included on‑chain — at every decision time, even past 6s. (Overall ~0.06% of attestations do miss, but each of those is a committee that never reached a decided attestation — a failure mode upstream of, and independent of, decision timing; see method.) What deciding late actually correlates with is head‑vote correctness: it holds at ~100% until 4s, drops sharply to ~95.5% in the 4–5.5s window (a late decision usually means a late block, so the vote recorded a stale head), then recovers past 6s as round‑2 refetches fresh data.

Why inclusion survives what would kill a proposal

A block that lands after ~4s loses the fork‑choice race and is orphaned. An attestation has no such cliff — it can be included up to 32 slots later, and here it essentially always makes the very next block. So the committee can decide at 5s, even 6s, and the attestation still lands on‑chain; it just may have recorded a stale head vote if it decided in the 4–6s round‑1‑late window.

Phase 1 · round-1 anatomy

Late start, near‑instant consensus

The lateness is entirely in the start, not the consensus. Once the round‑1 leader proposes — median 2.3s into the slot, waiting on the block and attestation data — the committee reaches a decided quorum in a median of just 44ms: 60% land within 100ms of the proposal, 89% within 250ms. A committee‑duty's decision time is therefore essentially its start time plus a near‑zero tail — the QBFT rounds themselves are cheap.

Two kinds of slow committee

The genuine tail splits in two. Some slow committees are the same chronic round‑changers (e.g. af171fec: 74 slow round‑1s and 61 round‑changes) — they crawl and often tip over the 6s edge into round 2. Others are slow but steady (5862a52a: 75 slow round‑1s but 0 round‑changes across 936 duties; d40e914: 70 and 0) — a persistently‑slow‑but‑alive operator that always drags round‑1 out to 1–2s yet still lands inside the budget. Neither costs inclusion.

Rounds

Round 2 is benign here — the opposite of proposer

Round changes are more common than for proposals (~1.1% vs 0.17%), but for attestations they're harmless and even helpful: round‑2 duties still hit 100% inclusion and their round‑2 refetch restores head‑vote correctness. For a proposer, round 2 meant a 14% miss rate.

Why round changes are ~6× more common than for proposals

  • A few unhealthy committees dominate. The 1.09% rate is not uniform: 113 of 331 committees ever round‑change, and the worst 10 account for 60% of all round changes — several sit at 25–29% (e.g. 61/210, 240/959 of their own duties). This is operator health in specific clusters, not a network‑wide property.
  • A structural timer difference makes committee round‑1 tighter. In the SSV code the committee round‑1 timer fires at a fixed slotStart + 6s, whereas the proposer's is instance‑relative — a full 2s from consensus start (SSV issue #2429). Committees start consensus late (median 2.3s, p99 4.2s into slot), so their effective round‑1 budget is only 6s − start: ~3.7s typically, but under 2s and shrinking for late starters. The same operator slowness blows a shrinking budget far more easily than the proposer's always‑fresh 2s.
  • Late blocks add a systemic nudge. The round‑change rate roughly doubles (1.0% → ~2.0%) at slots where committees decide late, and clusters — up to 13% of a slot's committees round‑change together. A late or contested block leaves operators transiently disagreeing on the head, so round‑1 misses its prepare‑quorum; round‑2's refetch then agrees — which is exactly why head‑vote correctness recovers past 6s.

Method & caveats

  • Unit. One QBFT per (committee, slot) from the Exporter committee trace (decided round + time); on‑chain outcome (inclusion, head vote, inclusion distance) per validator from E2M, rolled up per committee‑duty. 111,470 committee‑duties, 1.84M validator‑attestations.
  • Sampling. Stratified one epoch per day over 30 days (seeded) — attester duties are ~1000× proposer volume, so full enumeration isn't practical. This mirrors the committee sampling in ssvlabs/ssv #2883.
  • Decision time is the Exporter's receive‑time of the aggregated decided message, relative to slot start. The committee round‑1 timer is slot‑anchored at slotDuration/3 + 2s = 6s; round 2 at 8s.
  • No ProposerDelay / no drift lens. ProposerDelay is proposer‑only, and committee duties do no MEV block‑fetch, so operator‑start drift is smaller and less interesting here — round‑1 consensus itself is fast (median 44ms). The late decisions are late starts (waiting on attestation data / head‑start), not slow consensus.
  • Head vote reflects the head the committee saw when it built the attestation data; a late decision correlates with late data and a stale head. The 4–5s dip is therefore a common‑cause effect (a late/absent block drives both), not the QBFT decision itself corrupting the vote. Inclusion and inclusion‑distance come from E2M's on‑chain view.
  • Inclusion is conditional on a decided trace. The dataset is keyed on Exporter committee traces, so it contains only attestations the committee actually decided and submitted — hence 0 non‑inclusions among the 1.84M analysed. Measured directly from E2M over the same 30 epochs, the overall attestation rate is 99.937% included / 0.063% missed (1,169 of 1,855,047) and 99.65% correct head vote. Every one of those 1,169 misses is absent from the decided traces — i.e. the committee never produced a decided attestation, a failure mode upstream of and independent of decision timing. So "a decided attestation always lands" is the precise claim, not "no attestation was ever missed."
